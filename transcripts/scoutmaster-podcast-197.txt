A perennial difficulty that Scouters run into is the evaluation of the positions of
responsibility that are part of the Star Life and Eagle ranks. In one way, what I'm about to talk
about is very specific advice for those particular ranks in the Boy Scouts of America. But I think
there is a lot of things in general that we can apply what I'm about to say in Scouting.
We need to remember that evaluation of things like this in Scouting are not like anything else.
And that's why we talk about it a lot. And that's why it's hard to get.
Our work and our professional lives as adults are attached to some sort of objective standard
about 99% of the time. Our Scouts in their lives, in school and in the things that they do in their
lives, are attached to some kind of objective standard. They get grades and things like that.
And when we get to Scouts, there are things that just don't have an objective standard.
And evaluating a position of responsibility is one of those things that causes problems sometimes.
So I want to take some time and talk about it because I get questions from adults who feel
like Scouts are skating by in their position of responsibility. They're not doing their job.
They get to the point where they evaluate that position of responsibility requirement for
star life and eagle and questions come up and it can cause a great deal of trouble.
But first of all, let's remember that positions of responsibility, leadership positions in Scouting
for youth are opportunities. How can somebody skate by on one of these positions? I just
don't understand how it can happen. How long before someone like the Scout Master, this is a
specific responsibility of a Scout Master, talks to the young man and gets them to up their game.
How can we get to the point where a Scout takes on a position of responsibility and then some
months later the judgment comes that he didn't do very well. That means to me the Scout has
not failed, but the Scouters who are involved with the Scout have failed. I mean, have they talked
to the Scout during that period? Does the Scout understand what their position of
responsibility is? Do they know what to do? Do they understand that things aren't going so well?
Has anybody brought that to their attention? If they aren't showing up and not fulfilling
the responsibility, why is that? On positions of responsibility, we just don't start a clock
and wait until it runs out before we're giving Scouts feedback and training and mentoring to
help them make the best of that opportunity. There should be no question in anyone's mind
when a Scout has completed his tenure in a position of responsibility that he's done his best
because this is a subject of frequent and meaningful discussion with the Scout Master.
It's that simple. How does the Scout Master approach this? Well, I suggest you go way overboard
in pointing out what a wonderful job the Scout did. You do this honestly and frequently in front
of the Patrol Leaders Council, in front of your fellow Scouters, and in front of the other Scouts
and watch what happens next. Every Scout with a position of responsibility will be paying
attention. They'll hear about the successes of their fellow Scouts and they will up their game.
If someone is actually kind of skating by, we find something positive about what they are doing
and we build on it. When I sit down and talk to a Scout where I am signing off their tenure in a
position of responsibility, I ask lots of questions. I ask them if they felt that they understood
what was expected of them. And the answer usually is, well, not at first, but I began to get the
hang of it as time went by. I ask them to give me an example of what they were proud of about
their work, if there was anything that they regretted or that they would do differently.
I ask them if they think they met that requirement and what they plan to do next.
It is almost certain that there will always be at least one Scout in a position of responsibility
that we'll look at and we will consider to be kind of lackluster in performance and kind of
reluctant. Rather than concentrating on what they aren't doing, look for the 10% or the 5% that they
are getting right and build up on it. Now, if you do that and they go from 5 or 10% effort to 50%
effort during the time they spend in that position of responsibility, haven't we succeeded? Aren't
we getting out of scouting what we want to get out of it? Are we looking for progress or are we looking
for some certain standard of performance? When it comes to evaluating a position of responsibility,
there are no objective standards other than the individual Scout's effort. Let me read this
section to you from the new book, okay, The Scouting Journey. Imagine achieving a subjective
requirement was a literal foot race and the finish line is an ideal standard. Not all Scouts begin
that race at the starting line. They begin at different places. They have different home lives,
characters and habits and these place them ahead of or behind their fellow Scouts.
Our average Scout with no outstanding advantages or deficits begins the race, let's say,
at the starting line. Scouts with greater advantages and skills in a given area
begin 75 yards ahead of the starting line. Scouts with little or no advantage or skill
or some real deficits begin 50 yards behind the starting line. The starter's pistol
sounds, the Scouts are off and running and our gifted Scouts cover the short distance to the
finish line quickly. An average Scout covers 75% of the course, another covers 25% of the course.
The Scout who began the race well behind the starting line just reaches the starting line
before the race is over. So the question is, how much ground did each Scout cover?
The Scout who began farthest behind and only reached the starting line has progressed
further than the gifted Scout who reached the finish line. Our aim will always be the ideal
at the finish line but our evaluation is about progress. Now this aspect of Scouting is difficult
for a lot of us. It's very right in theory to require everyone reach the same finish line
but that's not what we are after. We want all of our Scouts to progress. This is why subjective
requirements don't have objective standards. They're based on individual progress.
Now absolutely not everyone thinks I have this right. They think we should set rigorous standards
and only reward Scouts who reach the ideal at the finish line. I'd agree with them if all of
our Scouts started at the same point but they don't. They just don't. When called on to
make a subjective evaluation like this we should ask our Scouts to evaluate themselves using their
own internal standard. The answer gives us all kinds of opportunities to strengthen how they think,
how they see themselves, and how they encourage themselves to do better. When Scouts evaluate
themselves against their own best efforts rather than against the efforts of others
or some objective standard they learn to find inner motivation and purpose. When we help them
exercise this internal standard we enable Scouts to weave the Scout oath and law into their life
and character. They form a personal connection to the moral and ethical precepts of Scouting.
They learn that their internal motivations are more important than how others see us.
This type of thing to me is so very important. It's everything that we're aimed at as Scouters.
Developing people of character who know how to encourage themselves, who know how to look at
their efforts honestly, and who have a positive outlook on life that's informed by the Scout
oath and law. If you're finding evaluating these kind of subjective things like positions of
responsibility like Scout Spirit and the other requirements that seem to cause a lot of trouble,
just remember we are in the business of development, not performance measurement.
We want all of our Scouts to progress, but more importantly we want them to learn those valuable
life skills embodied in the Scout oath and law by developing an internal standard by which
they can judge their own performance, encourage themselves, and continue to do better.
